FAQ

Before you book

When should you rebuild vs. modernize a legacy system?

Rebuild when the architecture is fundamentally incompatible with where the business is going. Modernize when the core logic is sound and the bottlenecks are in specific layers — the database, API layer, or UI. Most CTOs overestimate the need to rebuild and underestimate how long it takes. The right test: can you clearly describe the new system in terms of the current business model? If not, the business needs to stabilize before the architecture can.

How long does a full legacy system replacement actually take?

Honest answer: 18–36 months for enterprise-scale systems. Most estimates are wrong because they don't account for data migration, integration testing, and user retraining. Budget for 2x what engineering estimates. The teams that succeed are the ones that account for this upfront, not the ones that discover it mid-project.

What are the clear signs a legacy system needs replacing rather than refactoring?

Replace when: (1) the tech stack can't be staffed because the skill set is too rare, (2) adding features requires understanding 10-year-old business logic that nobody can explain, or (3) the system processes data in a fundamentally different model than your business operates today. Refactor when the logic is sound and specific layers are the bottleneck.

What is the cost of doing nothing with a legacy system?

You're already paying it — in slower delivery, higher engineering turnover, and accumulated workarounds that make every project more expensive. The question isn't whether to pay; it's whether you pay now with a plan or later in a crisis. Unplanned legacy failure typically costs 3–5x more than planned modernization.
