Client Background

The Office of Equity had to make GenAI accountable before adoption moved too far

The Washington State Office of Equity was established to embed equity across state government and reduce disparities in how systems serve Washingtonians, especially historically marginalized communities.

In January 2024, Executive Order 24-01 required the Office to lead development of a framework for fair, ethical, and equitable use of Generative Artificial Intelligence across state agencies.

The work had to balance technical risk, public trust, agency usability, procurement needs, legal safeguards, and community voice. The goal was not simply to approve or reject GenAI. The goal was to create a model for responsible use.

Challenges

The state needed guidance that worked for agencies and communities

GenAI adoption was moving quickly, but the accountability structure was still being formed.

  1. No existing accountability model. The Office needed a practical structure for equitable GenAI use where no mature statewide model existed yet.
  2. Agency practices were inconsistent. State agencies operated in silos, with different levels of AI maturity, different risk assumptions, and no unified monitoring structure.
  3. Communities had real trust concerns. Community members expressed concern about AI in government, especially where tools might affect law enforcement, access to services, or public decision-making.
  4. The deadline was fixed. The Office needed actionable guidance by September 2024 under the executive mandate.
  5. The language had to be both technical and accessible. The Blueprint needed enough detail for implementation while staying clear enough for public understanding and agency use.

Lessons Learned

AI governance only works when it connects people, policy, and implementation

The Blueprint showed that responsible GenAI requires plain language, technical safeguards, procurement discipline, and community participation.

  1. Plain-language governance matters. Agencies need guidance that is understandable, actionable, and usable across different technical maturity levels.
  2. Community engagement cannot be symbolic. Community insight should inform technical development, policy choices, risk review, and feedback loops.
  3. Bias mitigation must be continuous. Bias checks, indicators, drift monitoring, and correction mechanisms help keep AI accountability active after launch.
  4. Procurement is part of AI governance. Vendor requirements, rejection rationale, due diligence, and compliance records determine whether responsible AI survives the buying process.
  5. Repeatability builds trust. Consistent Output Protocol and fairness controls support AI systems that are easier to explain, audit, and improve.
