Nobody Wants a Pitch They Want to Be Understood First

Cold outreach fails for a predictable reason: it opens with the sender's product instead of the recipient's problem. Taliferro builds the Outreach app precisely to make the second version easier to send at scale — but the underlying skill is one anyone reaching out to a stranger can learn.

Introduction

Most cold outreach opens with the sender: their product, their offer, their pitch. It gets ignored for the same reason a stranger interrupting you on the street gets ignored — it's about them, not you. The messages that actually get a reply open somewhere else entirely: with the recipient's situation.

Lead With Their Perspective

Before you say anything about what you're offering, understand what the person you're reaching out to is actually dealing with. Ask questions if you can. Read what they've written or posted if you can't. A message that shows you understand their situation earns a reply before it ever mentions your product.

Benefits, Not Features

A feature list is information about your product. A benefit is what changes for them. Lead with the second one — it's what they're actually deciding on, whether or not they'd describe it that way.

Use a Real Story, Not a Script

The most persuasive thing you can send a stranger isn't a pitch — it's a specific, true story about a problem you solved for someone like them. Not a testimonial quote engineered for a landing page; the actual sequence of what was broken, what you did, and what changed. Specificity is what makes it believable.

Handle Competitor Questions Honestly

If someone asks how you compare to another company, answer the actual question — differences in approach, scope, or fit — instead of running down the competitor. Prospects notice when a pitch turns into a takedown, and it costs more trust than it buys. This applies well beyond sales conversations; it's just how you talk about anyone you're not in the room with.

Know When to Walk Away

Ask if it's a good time before launching into anything. Keep it short. If they say no, take the no — respectfully, without a second and third attempt. A gracious no today doesn't close the door permanently, and pushing past it is what closes doors that would have otherwise reopened later.

The same applies after a follow-up gets declined: say thanks, ask if it's alright to check back down the line, and leave it there. This is the exact discipline Taliferro built into the Outreach app — sequencing and cadence that respect a "not now" instead of grinding through it.

Conclusion

Engaging a stranger about your product isn't a persuasion problem — it's an attention problem, and attention goes to whoever demonstrates they understand the person first. Lead with their situation, back it with a real story, and respect the answer you get, and the "pitch" mostly takes care of itself.
