Build What Customers Can't Live Without

A product can ship on time, pass every review, and still be something customers merely tolerate instead of need. Taliferro treats technology leadership as customer listening translated into action — the team builds what people actually asked for, not what's easiest to ship. The team that gets closest to the customer, and moves fastest on what they hear, usually wins. Everything else is management theater.

TS Tyrone Showers Co-Founder Taliferro · Sep 14, 2022 · 2 min read

The most important thing a technology leader can do is find out what the company is actually trying to achieve, and then figure out how to help with that specific goal — not a generic version of "innovation" or "productivity." It's the same discipline Taliferro applies to its own delivery work: understand the actual goal before writing a line of code.

Start with the customer, not the roadmap

You're in business to help customers, and the roadmap should reflect what they're actually asking for — not just what's easiest to build next. That means actively seeking out feedback, not waiting for it to arrive as a complaint. If a customer wants something the product doesn't do yet, the job is figuring out how to add it without breaking what already works, not filing it away and moving on.

Hire people better than you

Hire people who are smarter than you, more experienced than you in their specific area, and different from you — different skill sets, different backgrounds, different ways of thinking about a problem. A team that's a copy of the leader has a copy of the leader's blind spots. The point of hiring well isn't ego management. It's making sure the team can see what you can't.

Then keep investing in them. Ask questions, listen to their ideas, and give people real opportunities to grow — a team that never gets room to grow eventually stagnates and starts looking for the door.

Communication is the actual job

Deep communication isn't measured in word count. It's a two-way conversation where both sides are actually listening, not just waiting to talk — and it's practiced consistently, not just when something's gone wrong. A weekly 1:1 between a leader and each direct report does more for alignment than any all-hands meeting, because it gives people room to ask questions and raise concerns without competing for airtime.

Being technical helps here — it gives you the credibility to jump in and help when someone's stuck, and the ability to explain what's actually happening instead of translating through someone else. But the technical skill only matters if you're also leading by example: showing the standard you expect instead of just stating it.

None of this is a science. It's closer to a practice — the leaders worth learning from are the ones still experimenting with what works for their specific team, not the ones who found one formula and stopped adjusting.
