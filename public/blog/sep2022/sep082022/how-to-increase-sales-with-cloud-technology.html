The Cloud Isn't One Thing Stop Treating It Like One

SaaS, PaaS, and IaaS get lumped together as "the cloud" as if picking one solves the same problem as picking another — they don't. Taliferro treats each layer as a different decision with a different cost and a different amount of control you give up. Picking the right layer up front saves more time than any tool inside it ever will.

TS Tyrone Showers Co-Founder Taliferro · Sep 8, 2022 · 2 min read

When cloud complexity starts slowing delivery, how we untangle cloud systems shows how Taliferro turns cloud architecture into working execution, and the Momentum System keeps the work tied to outcomes instead of activity.

Three different things called "the cloud"

Cloud computing breaks down into three layers, and confusing them is where most cloud decisions go wrong:

  • SaaS (Software as a Service) — accessed through a browser, nothing to install. You're renting the finished application.
  • PaaS (Platform as a Service) — development tools for building your own apps, without managing the infrastructure underneath.
  • IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) — virtualized servers you configure yourself, for hosting a website or a custom service.

Each one trades control for convenience differently. SaaS gives you the least control and the fastest start. IaaS gives you the most control and the most responsibility. Picking the wrong layer for the job is how companies end up either over-engineering a simple need or hitting a wall they can't customize their way around.

What you actually gain

Done right, cloud infrastructure lets your systems talk to each other in real time instead of waiting on manual updates or an IT ticket. Nobody has to install anything locally or learn a new tool from scratch. And the cost model flips: you pay for what you use instead of licensing software upfront whether anyone touches it or not — which matters more as a team grows, since adding people doesn't mean buying more hardware.

There's a security upside too: data stored offsite through a reputable provider is generally a smaller target than a server sitting in a back office nobody's patched in months.

Where personalization and mobile fit in

The same infrastructure that powers real-time integration also powers personalized customer experience — collecting and analyzing behavior well enough to know a visitor who keeps returning without buying should get a different message than someone buying for the first time. And it's what makes the everyday collaboration stack — Gmail, Calendar, Drive, a CRM, an e-commerce platform — work together instead of as five disconnected logins.

None of that requires understanding every service on the market. It requires picking the right layer for the actual problem, which is a smaller and much more answerable question.
