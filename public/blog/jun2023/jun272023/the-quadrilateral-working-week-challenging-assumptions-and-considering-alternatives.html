The Four-Day Week Sounds Great Until You Read the Fine Print

A four-day week sounds like an easy win for talent retention, but Gartner analyst Jordan Turner's research shows the picture is more mixed than the headlines suggest. Here's a clear-eyed look at what the UK trial actually found, and why a flexible approach usually beats a wholesale switch.

TS Tyrone Showers Co-Founder Taliferro · Jun 27, 2023 · 2 min read

Introduction

Gartner analyst Jordan Turner looked at why the four-day work week is picking up momentum as a talent retention play. It's a genuinely appealing idea, but the practicality and tradeoffs deserve more scrutiny than the pitch usually gets — and there are alternatives that can deliver similar benefits with less disruption.

Questioning the Efficacy

The UK trial Turner references did show positive results, but it's not automatically a fit for every organization. The stress reduction and better work-life balance employees reported have to be weighed against real downsides — overtime pay implications, and the fatigue that can come from compressing the same workload into longer shifts. It's also worth noting the study measured a 1.4% average revenue improvement, which is a modest number to base a full organizational restructuring on.

Alternative Work Structures

Rather than jumping straight to a four-day week, it's worth looking at other structures that offer flexibility without cutting the number of workdays. Flexible hours, remote work, and compressed schedules (four 10-hour shifts, for example) can deliver a lot of the same benefit — better balance, less burnout — without the full operational disruption of a shorter week. Tailoring the approach to what a specific workforce actually needs tends to hold up better than a one-size-fits-all mandate.

Considerations for Implementation

Rolling out a four-day week takes real operational planning — it's not a policy change that implements itself. Business continuity and fair treatment across the workforce both need to be worked out ahead of time, not assumed. HR leaders and managers should assess feasibility honestly rather than assuming the upside is automatic. Starting with a small pilot and expanding gradually gives an organization the chance to catch problems, gather real feedback, and adjust before making any permanent change.

Individual Preferences and Voluntary Opt-Out

A four-day week appeals to a lot of employees, but not everyone wants it or can make it work for their circumstances. Organizations should respect that and offer it as an opt-in rather than a mandate. Some employees genuinely do better on a traditional schedule, and forcing a single structure on everyone defeats the point of trying to improve work-life balance in the first place.

Looking Beyond the Four-Day Week

The four-day week shouldn't be the only lever an organization pulls for employee well-being, work-life balance, and retention. Better tooling, a genuinely flexible culture, real paths for professional development, and a supportive work environment can produce similar gains without the disruption of restructuring the entire workweek.

Conclusion

The four-day week is gaining traction for good reason, but it deserves scrutiny, not automatic adoption. Question whether it actually fits, look at the alternatives, and roll out changes cautiously — that's what leads to decisions that work for employees and the business, rather than decisions made because a trend is popular.
