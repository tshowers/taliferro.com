What Forrester's Loyalty Research Actually Proves

Forrester's research on customer loyalty points to three specific, measurable levers — not vague "customer-centricity" advice. Taliferro breaks down what the research actually found and what it means in practice.

TS Tyrone Showers Co-Founder Taliferro · Jun 10, 2023 · 1 min read

Introduction

Forrester Research, led by VP and Principal Analyst Maxie Schmidt, studied what actually drives customer loyalty in January 2021. Three factors stood out with specific, measurable effects: how fast problems get resolved, how clearly a company communicates, and how knowledgeable its employees are. Each one moved a different outcome — retention, spending, or advocacy.

Fast Problem Resolution Drives Retention

Customers were 2.4 times more likely to stay loyal to companies that resolved their issues quickly. Speed here isn't just convenience — a fast response signals that the company is actually paying attention, which builds the kind of trust that turns a transaction into an ongoing relationship.

Clear Communication Drives Spending

Companies that communicated clearly and concisely saw customers 2.7 times more likely to increase their spending. Ambiguity creates hesitation — a customer who isn't sure what they're getting or why it matters spends less. Clear communication removes that friction and makes the value of what's being offered obvious enough to act on.

Knowledgeable Employees Drive Advocacy

The strongest effect in the study: when in-person staff could fully answer customer questions, those customers were 10 times more likely to recommend the company. That's a much bigger multiplier than the other two factors, and it makes sense — a customer who gets a real answer from someone who clearly knows the product becomes a source of free, credible word-of-mouth in a way a fast reply or a clear email never quite does.

Conclusion

Three specific, measurable factors — not a vague commitment to "customer-centricity" — are what the research shows actually move loyalty, spending, and advocacy: how fast problems get fixed, how clearly a company communicates, and how well its people actually know the product. Taliferro treats these as concrete inputs to design around, not abstractions to aspire to.
