It's Not "Slow" It's the Specific Second Where Customers Leave

"Speed matters" isn't useful because it has no threshold. Google's own research does: as load time goes from 1 to 3 seconds, the probability a visitor bounces rises 32%. From 1 to 5 seconds, 90%. From 1 to 10 seconds, 123%. Amazon's famous $1.6 billion figure is what that math looks like at Amazon's scale — the same curve applies to a store with a fraction of the traffic.

TS Tyrone Showers Co-Founder Taliferro · Oct 22, 2022 · 2 min read

Introduction

"Site speed matters" is one of the most repeated pieces of advice in e-commerce, and one of the least specific. It doesn't say what "slow" means, or what happens the moment a page crosses from acceptable to costly. There are actual numbers behind this, from Google's own research on real user behavior, and they're more useful than the general advice because they tell you exactly where the cliff is.

The Actual Numbers

Google's mobile research on page-speed and bounce rate is specific: as load time increases from 1 second to 3 seconds, the probability of a visitor bouncing increases 32%. From 1 to 5 seconds, it's 90%. From 1 to 6 seconds, 106%. From 1 to 10 seconds, 123%. The curve isn't linear — the cost of each additional second gets worse, not better, the longer a visitor waits. That's why "our site loads in 4 seconds, that's not bad" is the wrong framing: 4 seconds already sits well past the point where a large share of visitors have decided to leave.

Why the Threshold Feels Sudden

There's a decades-old rule from usability research (Jakob Nielsen's response-time limits) that explains why: a response within 0.1 seconds feels instantaneous, one within 1 second feels like part of a continuous action even though the delay is noticeable, and past about 10 seconds a visitor's attention has left the task entirely — they've assumed something is broken or gone to check email. Your page doesn't need to hit that 10-second mark to lose someone; by the 3-second mark, you're already deep into the range where people quietly give up and click back.

Where This Actually Gets Fixed

Knowing the threshold matters is only useful once you know where your specific page is losing time — a real case where most of a 60% speed gain came from removing things, not adding them, and a walkthrough of how to actually measure where the time is going using the tool built into every modern browser. If you're on a platform like Shopify, every widget you've installed has a measurable cost worth checking against this same 1-to-3-second window.

Conclusion

"Fast enough" isn't a feeling, it's a number with real research behind it, and the number is smaller than most sites assume. One extra second isn't a minor inconvenience — it's the specific point on the curve where a meaningful share of your visitors have already decided to leave.
