Most SharePoint Redesigns Fail Before a Single Page Gets Rebuilt

Teams almost always start a SharePoint redesign by picking a template, then wonder why adoption stalls six months later. Taliferro lays out the assessment, planning, and stakeholder work that has to happen before the visual redesign — because that's usually where the real failure starts.

Published: 12 Jul 2023 · Updated: 4 Sep 2026

By Tyrone Showers

Article

Introduction

SharePoint redesigns fail more often at the planning stage than the execution stage. A team picks a new template, moves the same disorganized content into it, and six months later adoption looks exactly like it did before — because the actual problems (unclear objectives, no stakeholder input, messy information architecture) never got addressed. Here's the order that actually works.

Assess Your Current State

Before touching the design, find out what's actually broken — pull real user feedback, check which pages get used and which are ghost towns, and look at performance complaints. A redesign built on assumptions instead of this kind of evidence tends to fix problems nobody had while leaving the real ones untouched.

Define Clear Objectives

"Make it better" isn't an objective. Improving user experience, fixing document management, or tightening security are each different projects with different priorities — naming the specific goal up front is what keeps a redesign from turning into a scope-creeping visual refresh that solves nothing.

Engage Key Stakeholders

The people who'll use the redesigned site daily know things a project team doesn't — which workflows are actually painful, which features get ignored, what they've given up trying to find. Interviews, surveys, and short workshops with actual end-users, not just department heads, are what keep the redesign grounded in real usage instead of leadership's mental model of how the site gets used.

Information Architecture Matters

A prettier interface on top of the same disorganized content structure fixes nothing — most SharePoint frustration is people not being able to find things, not the visual design. Logical content organization and consistent metadata tagging are what make search actually work, which matters more to daily users than any layout choice.

Responsive Design for Accessibility

A meaningful share of SharePoint access now happens on a phone or tablet, so a responsive design that adapts cleanly to different screen sizes isn't optional anymore — a site that only works well on desktop is failing a growing share of the people trying to use it.

Focus on User Experience (UX)

Adoption lives or dies on whether people can do the basics — upload a document, find something, collaborate on a file — without hunting for the right menu. An intuitive layout and clear navigation aren't cosmetic details; they're the actual reason a redesign gets used or gets ignored in favor of emailing files back and forth like before.

Security and Compliance

A redesign is the natural moment to review security settings, permissions, and compliance features that have drifted since the last setup — stale access grants, missing encryption, no multi-factor authentication. Fixing these during the redesign is far cheaper than discovering the gap during an audit or a breach.

Customization and Personalization

Different teams use SharePoint differently, and a rigid one-size-fits-all layout ignores that. Letting users personalize dashboards and surface the content relevant to their role — using SharePoint's own web parts and templates — is what makes the platform feel built for the people using it instead of imposed on them.

Content Migration Strategy

Moving content during a redesign without a real plan is how files go missing, permissions break, and version history gets lost. A migration plan with data integrity checks and a rollback path is what keeps the transition from becoming its own crisis.

Training and Adoption Strategy

Shipping the redesign is half the job — if people don't understand what changed or why, they'll route around it. A real training plan that explains the new features and directly addresses "why did you move my stuff" is what actually converts a redesign into adoption instead of quiet resentment.

Regular Maintenance and Updates

A SharePoint redesign isn't a one-time project that's "done" at launch — it needs ongoing review as usage patterns shift and Microsoft ships new features. Treating it as finished the day it launches is how it drifts right back into the state that triggered the redesign in the first place.

Conclusion

A SharePoint redesign succeeds or fails based on the work done before anyone touches a template — assessment, clear objectives, and real stakeholder input. Skip those, and even a beautifully designed site inherits the same problems as the one it replaced: improved user experience, stronger security, and real productivity gains all depend on getting that groundwork right first.
