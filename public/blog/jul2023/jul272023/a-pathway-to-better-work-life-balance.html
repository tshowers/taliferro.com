Your Team's Long Hours Are Usually an Operations Problem

When a team is chronically working late, the instinct is to hire more people or ask for more hours. Taliferro's experience says look at the process first — manual work that should have been automated is a far more common cause than an actual staffing shortage.

TS Tyrone Showers Co-Founder Taliferro · Jul 27, 2023 · 2 min read

Introduction

Chasing profitability tends to push operational efficiency down the priority list, and long hours are usually the first symptom that shows up. The cost lands on employees' personal time, not the balance sheet, which is exactly why it's easy for leadership to miss. Technology already exists to fix most of this — the gap is usually investment and attention, not capability.

The Dilemma of Operational Inefficiency

Operational inefficiency is a quiet drain — tasks that could be automated keep getting done manually, and the extra hours that takes bleed into personal and family time. That slow accumulation of overtime is one of the more common, underdiagnosed causes of burnout, precisely because it doesn't look like a crisis day to day.

The Need for Operational Efficiency

Operational efficiency deserves to be treated as a top business priority, not a nice-to-have. AI, machine learning, and ERP systems can take over the repetitive parts of a workflow, cutting error rates and freeing up real time — not hypothetically, but measurably.

The direct effect is better time management: when tasks that used to take hours manually run automatically instead, employees stop needing to work through evenings just to keep pace. That time doesn't disappear — it goes back to people's actual lives.

The Role of Leadership

Leadership sets the tone here. Actively championing operational efficiency isn't just a performance play — it's a visible signal that a company takes its employees' time seriously, which shows up in retention and morale more reliably than most engagement initiatives do.

It benefits leadership directly too — smoother operations mean fewer fires to put out and fewer administrative tasks eating into a leader's own time, which is its own version of the same problem playing out at the top of the org chart.

Conclusion

Operational efficiency isn't just a cost-cutting exercise — done right, it gives employees back time for family, rest, and personal growth that chronic overtime otherwise takes from them.

Companies that actually invest in fixing the operational drag build something more durable than a better bottom line — they build a workplace people don't need to escape from, which is what makes the business sustainable in the first place.
