More technology does not mean less work

Digital efficiency begins when technology removes effort instead of adding another system to manage. Taliferro explains how better processes, practical tools, and the right partners can lower costs, improve response time, and help a business adapt without creating more friction.

TS Tyrone Showers Co-Founder Taliferro · Dec 15, 2022 · 2 min read

What actually counts as efficient

A new tool can make things worse before it makes them better — or just worse, full stop. A team adopts software to "improve efficiency," and six months later someone is manually copying data between the new system and the old one, because nobody actually turned the old one off. That's not digital efficiency. That's two jobs where there used to be one.

Being digitally efficient doesn't mean owning more digital tools. It means fewer steps between a customer's request and a resolved outcome, less information typed twice into two different systems, and less time spent explaining the current state of work instead of moving it forward.

We built TODD around exactly this problem. It attaches to signals you already have — emails, calls, notes — instead of asking a team to enter data into yet another place. The standard we held ourselves to was simple: if it doesn't remove a step, it doesn't ship.

Where it actually pays off

The value shows up hardest in two moments: when demand spikes and when the market turns. A retailer that can reroute inventory the same day a supplier falls through is running efficiently. One that finds out three weeks later, from a spreadsheet nobody updated, is not — no matter how much software both companies own.

The difference was never the number of tools. It's whether the process behind them was ever actually fixed, or whether new software just got layered on top of an old, broken one to compensate for it.

The tool is not the strategy

Software doesn't run itself. The best CRM, the best automation platform, the best AI feature — all of it depends on someone deciding what "ready" looks like, who owns the handoff, and when to bring in outside help instead of trying to build everything in-house.

That's the combination that actually produces efficiency: the right tool, a process that fits how the team really works, and a partner brought in for the parts that aren't your team's core skill — not another subscription added to the pile.

The takeaway

More technology does not mean less work. It means less work only when it replaces a manual step instead of sitting next to it. Before adding another tool, ask what specific step it removes — not what it adds.
