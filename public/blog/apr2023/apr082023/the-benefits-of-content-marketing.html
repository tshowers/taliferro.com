Most Content Marketing Advice Describes Content Marketing — It Doesn't Do It

A post that could be about any company isn't doing its job, no matter how many keywords made it in. We know because a chunk of Taliferro's own blog looked exactly like that — this is what we learned rewriting it.

Introduction

Search "benefits of content marketing" and you'll get a hundred versions of the same post: content marketing builds visibility, drives engagement, costs less than ads, wins long-term trust. All true. All useless, because the post proving those claims is itself generic, keyword-stuffed, and says nothing a reader couldn't guess before clicking. If content marketing works by demonstrating expertise, a post that demonstrates nothing except the ability to restate a Wikipedia summary is doing the opposite of what it claims to do.

We know this because a chunk of our own blog looked exactly like that, and we're in the middle of rewriting it.

Specific Beats Comprehensive

A post covering "the benefits of X" in broad strokes reads like it was written to exist, not to answer a question a real person had. The posts that actually work answer one specific question precisely — "why does my JWT keep failing PKCE validation," not "the pros and cons of authentication." Comprehensiveness feels thorough; specificity is what's actually useful. Google's gotten much better at telling the difference, but readers could always tell.

If You Wouldn't Say It Out Loud, Don't Publish It

"Preeminent strategy," "engender brand awareness," "myriad benefits" — nobody talks like this, which is the tell. Inflated language isn't a style choice, it's usually a symptom: the writer didn't have a specific point to make, so the sentence got dressed up to sound like it was saying more than it was. Plain language makes a weak argument obvious immediately, which is exactly why weak arguments avoid it.

What Actually Works

The genuine advantage of content marketing over ads isn't cost, though it usually is cheaper. It's that a good piece of content demonstrates the expertise an ad can only claim. An ad says "we know what we're doing." A post that correctly explains a genuinely hard problem proves it — and unlike an ad, it keeps proving it for years, to anyone who finds it later. That compounding is the entire case for content marketing. Everything else is a footnote.

Which means the bar for publishing isn't "is this on-brand and keyword-rich." It's "does this teach the reader something they didn't already know, in language they'd actually use." Most of what gets published — including, historically, a fair amount of ours — doesn't clear that bar. We're going back through it folder by folder.

Conclusion

Content marketing works when the content is worth reading on its own, independent of who wrote it or what they're selling. If a post could be about any company, it's not doing its job — no matter how many of the "right" keywords made it in.
