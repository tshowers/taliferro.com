Most Website Failures Happen After Launch, Not Before

A site that launches clean and then goes untouched degrades on a predictable schedule — broken links, stale content, an analytics dashboard nobody checks. Before Taliferro ships a site, we name who owns it after launch, not just who built it.

Introduction

Every "avoid these web development pitfalls" article lists the same pre-launch checklist: plan well, go mobile-first, nail the SEO, test thoroughly. All correct, all necessary — and all beside the point, because the site that actually fails usually passed every item on that checklist at launch. It just never got touched again.

The Failures That Don't Show Up in a Demo

Mobile responsiveness tested by resizing a browser window misses the real problems — actual touch targets, actual load times on a real connection, actual behavior when a form autofills wrong. SEO built around keyword density misses that Google now reads for genuine topical depth, not repetition. Accessibility checked with an automated scanner misses what an actual screen-reader user hits in the first thirty seconds. Each of these looks "done" in a pre-launch review and fails the moment a real, specific user with a real, specific device or need shows up.

The Real Failure Mode Is Silence After Launch

A website that launches clean and then goes untouched degrades on a predictable schedule: broken links accumulate as linked pages move or disappear, content goes stale while the business it describes keeps changing, and nobody notices the analytics dashboard exists until a client asks why traffic dropped six months ago. The site wasn't built badly — it was built once and then abandoned to its own slow decay.

Who Actually Owns It After Launch?

Before launch, ask the question that actually predicts long-term success: who is checking this site's analytics next quarter? Who gets paged when a form stops submitting? If the honest answer is "nobody, we'll notice eventually," that's the real risk — bigger than any pre-launch checklist item, because it compounds silently for as long as nobody's watching.

Conclusion

Plan well, build for mobile, get the SEO and accessibility right — all still true. But treat launch as the start of the site's life, not the finish line, and put a name next to who owns what happens after. That single decision predicts more about a website's long-term success than any pre-launch checklist.
