Volunteering Is a Low-Risk Way to Test a New Passion

Changing careers is a big, expensive bet to make on a guess. Taliferro looks at why volunteering is the cheapest way to find out if a new field is actually for you before you stake a job on it.

TS Tyrone Showers Co-Founder Taliferro · Aug 7, 2023 · 2 min read

Introduction

Plenty of people find their real career by volunteering their way into it first. That's not a coincidence — volunteering is one of the only ways to test a new field, cause, or skill without quitting a job or paying tuition to find out you don't like it. Here's how Taliferro sees that path actually play out.

Why Passion Alone Isn't Enough

Passion is what makes someone willing to explore a new field in the first place — but passion by itself doesn't tell you whether you're any good at it, or whether you'd still like it once it's routine instead of novel. That only shows up through practice.

Where Volunteering Fits In

Volunteering — offering skills, time, and energy toward a cause, unpaid — is where passion gets tested against practice. It gives someone a low-stakes way to find out:

It Lets You Experiment Without the Risk

Volunteering lets someone try a new domain and build real skill in it, without the commercial pressure of a paycheck riding on immediate performance.

It Builds a Real Network

Volunteer work puts someone in the same room as people already working in that field, which opens doors that a cold job application usually can't.

It Turns Interest Into Contribution

Volunteering for a cause someone actually cares about converts personal interest into something concrete other people can see and benefit from.

It Builds the Resilience a New Field Requires

Volunteer work has its own real setbacks and constraints, and working through them builds the kind of resilience that later matters if that path turns into a business or a career change.

How Volunteering Turns Into a Career

The path from volunteering to a full career change tends to follow the same stages:

  • Discovery: finding a cause or field that actually matches personal interest, not just curiosity.
  • Engagement: volunteering enough to learn the field from the inside, not just observe it.
  • Innovation: using the skills and relationships built there to solve a real problem in that space.
  • Transition: turning that experience into an actual job, business, or venture.

None of these stages is guaranteed or automatic — but skipping straight to a career change without going through them first is exactly what makes career pivots feel like such a gamble.

Conclusion

Volunteering isn't just a nice thing to put on a résumé — it's a legitimate, low-cost way to test whether a passion holds up once it becomes routine work. That's a real advantage over quitting a job or a program cold to chase something untested.

The people who make a real career change through volunteering usually aren't chasing a grand narrative about purpose — they're doing something much more practical: testing the work before betting a career on it.
